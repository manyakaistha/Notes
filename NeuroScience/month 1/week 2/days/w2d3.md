## 2. Topic 1: What is Filtering? (The Conceptual Goal)

A digital filter is a mathematical operator applied to a time series that modifies the relative amplitudes and phases of its frequency components. 

### The Golden Rule of Filtering
> [!CAUTION]
> **A filter does not "know" what is a brain wave and what is an artifact.** A filter operates strictly on **frequency (speed of oscillation)**, not biological origin. If a cranial muscle action potential and a genuine cognitive Gamma wave both oscillate at 60 Hz, linear filtering cannot separate them.

### Filter Terminology
- **Cutoff Frequency ($f_c$):** The boundary frequency where filter attenuation begins. In standard Butterworth designs, $f_c$ is defined as the **half-power point** where signal power drops by $50\%$ (amplitude attenuation of $1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707$, or $-3\text{ dB}$).
- **Passband:** The range of frequencies transmitted with minimal attenuation.
- **Stopband:** The range of frequencies suppressed below a specified gain threshold.
- **Transition Band:** The finite frequency zone connecting the passband and stopband. Real physical and digital filters cannot transition with infinite sharpness (brick-wall); they require a transition slope governed by the **filter order**.

## 3. Topic 2: The Four Fundamental Filters

| Filter Type | What Frequencies It Keeps (Passband) | What Frequencies It Attenuates (Stopband) | Canonical EEG Use Case | AI & Modeling Pitfall |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Low-Pass** | Frequencies below cutoff: $f < f_{\text{low}}$ | Frequencies above cutoff: $f > f_{\text{low}}$ | Removing high-frequency muscle fuzz (EMG) and radio/thermal noise (e.g., $f_c = 30\text{–}40\text{ Hz}$). | Aggressive low-pass filtering deletes the Gamma band ($30\text{–}80\text{ Hz}$), removing cognitive and sensory features. |
| **High-Pass** | Frequencies above cutoff: $f > f_{\text{high}}$ | Frequencies below cutoff: $f < f_{\text{high}}$ | Eliminating slow baseline drift from sweat and impedance shifts (e.g., $f_c = 0.5\text{–}1.0\text{ Hz}$). | Setting $f_c \ge 3\text{ Hz}$ suppresses physiological Delta waves ($0.5\text{–}4\text{ Hz}$), corrupting sleep and slow-wave analysis. |
| **Band-Pass** | Intermediate frequency range: $f_{\text{low}} < f < f_{\text{high}}$ | Everything below $f_{\text{low}}$ AND everything above $f_{\text{high}}$ | Isolating a single functional neural band (e.g., Alpha band-pass: $8\text{–}12\text{ Hz}$). | Eliminates all broadband cross-frequency coupling and contextual background dynamics. |
| **Notch (Band-Stop)** | All frequencies EXCEPT a very narrow notch: $|f - f_{\text{notch}}| > \Delta f$ | A surgically narrow band centered on target: $f_{\text{notch}} \pm \Delta f$ | Removing 50 Hz or 60 Hz electrical mains power-line interference (e.g., $49\text{–}51\text{ Hz}$). | Distorts phase and power of genuine neural oscillations occurring adjacent to mains frequency. |

## 4. Topic 3: Python Filtering Laboratory

Following our active learning methodology (**Concept → Code → Output → Interpretation**), we build a sequential filtering pipeline to clean a contaminated multi-component signal using SciPy Butterworth filters and zero-phase digital filtering.

### Why Zero-Phase (`scipy.signal.filtfilt`)?
A standard digital filter introduces a frequency-dependent **phase lag** (delaying certain frequencies more than others), which alters the temporal alignment of EEG peaks. SciPy's `filtfilt` executes a **forward-backward filtering pass**:
1. Passes the filter forward through the data.
2. Reverses the resulting array and filters it backward.
3. Reverses it once more.

This process exactly cancels all phase distortion (phase response $= 0^\circ$). 
*Consequence:* The effective filter order doubles (a 4th-order filter acts as an 8th-order filter), and cutoff attenuation becomes $-6\text{ dB}$ ($50\%$ amplitude gain, or $25\%$ power gain).

### Code Implementation (`filter_laboratory.py`)

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.signal import butter, filtfilt

# 1. Synthesize Contaminated Signal (4 seconds at 250 Hz)
np.random.seed(42)
fs = 250
t = np.linspace(0, 4, fs * 4, endpoint=False)
clean_alpha = 1.0 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 10 * t)         # 10 Hz Target Signal (1 uV peak)
slow_drift = 5.0 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 0.5 * t)         # 0.5 Hz Sweat/Movement Drift (5 uV)
line_noise = 3.0 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 50 * t)          # 50 Hz Power-line hum (3 uV)
high_freq_noise = np.random.normal(0, 1.5, len(t))     # Broadband sensor static
observed_eeg = clean_alpha + slow_drift + line_noise + high_freq_noise

# 2. Stage 1: 50 Hz Notch Filter (Band-stop 49-51 Hz)
b_notch, a_notch = butter(4, [49 / (fs/2), 51 / (fs/2)], btype='bandstop')
eeg_notch = filtfilt(b_notch, a_notch, observed_eeg)

# 3. Stage 2: 1 Hz High-Pass Filter (Removes 0.5 Hz sweat drift)
b_hp, a_hp = butter(4, 1.0 / (fs/2), btype='high')
eeg_hp = filtfilt(b_hp, a_hp, eeg_notch)

# 4. Stage 3: 30 Hz Low-Pass Filter (Removes high-frequency static)
b_lp, a_lp = butter(4, 30.0 / (fs/2), btype='low')
eeg_clean = filtfilt(b_lp, a_lp, eeg_hp)

# 5. Direct Alternative: 8-12 Hz Alpha Band-Pass Filter
b_bp, a_bp = butter(4, [8.0 / (fs/2), 12.0 / (fs/2)], btype='bandpass')
eeg_bandpass = filtfilt(b_bp, a_bp, observed_eeg)

# Quantitative Evaluation: Interior RMSE (1.0 s to 3.0 s, excluding edge transients)
interior = (t >= 1.0) & (t < 3.0)
for stage_name, data in [("Raw Observed", observed_eeg), 
                         ("Stage 1 (Notch)", eeg_notch), 
                         ("Stage 2 (High-Pass)", eeg_hp), 
                         ("Stage 3 (Clean / Low-Pass)", eeg_clean), 
                         ("Direct Band-Pass (8-12 Hz)", eeg_bandpass)]:
    rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean((data[interior] - clean_alpha[interior])**2))
    print(f"{stage_name:<28} Interior RMSE: {rmse:.3f} uV")
```

### Quantitative Output & Interpretation

```text
Raw Observed                 Interior RMSE: 4.298 uV
Stage 1 (Notch)              Interior RMSE: 3.792 uV
Stage 2 (High-Pass)          Interior RMSE: 1.475 uV
Stage 3 (Clean / Low-Pass)   Interior RMSE: 0.696 uV
Direct Band-Pass (8-12 Hz)   Interior RMSE: 0.230 uV
```

1. **Raw Observed ($\text{RMSE} = 4.298\mu\text{V}$):** The true 10 Hz signal ($A = 1\mu\text{V}$) is completely masked by the $5\mu\text{V}$ drift, $3\mu\text{V}$ mains hum, and random noise.
2. **Stage 1 Notch ($\text{RMSE} = 3.792\mu\text{V}$):** Strips out the 50 Hz carrier. The thick high-frequency line thinning confirms elimination of mains interference, though the slow 0.5 Hz undulating wave persists.
3. **Stage 2 High-Pass ($\text{RMSE} = 1.475\mu\text{V}$):** The high-pass filter eliminates the 0.5 Hz drift. The signal drops back to center symmetrically around $0\mu\text{V}$, reducing RMSE by more than $60\%$.
4. **Stage 3 Low-Pass ($\text{RMSE} = 0.696\mu\text{V}$):** Suppresses broadband high-frequency static. The waveform emerges as a pristine 10 Hz Alpha wave closely matching ground truth.
5. **Direct Band-Pass ($\text{RMSE} = 0.230\mu\text{V}$):** Restricting passband to $8\text{–}12\text{ Hz}$ provides the lowest RMSE because this synthetic target contains *only* a 10 Hz sine wave. In real biological data, an overly narrow band-pass discards meaningful cross-frequency dynamics.

## 5. Topic 4: The Danger of Preprocessing (Important Scientific Caveats)

Preprocessing is not harmless data cleaning; it alters the fundamental scientific hypothesis.

### 1. The Gamma Destruction Trap
If a researcher applies an aggressive 30 Hz low-pass filter to "clean up EMG noise," they permanently destroy all oscillatory information in the Gamma band ($30\text{–}80\text{ Hz}$). If the downstream AI model was intended to decode visual perception, working memory, or attention (which rely heavily on Gamma synchrony), the model will fail because the preprocessing eliminated the physiological mechanism.

### 2. The Delta Sleep Trap
If a researcher attempts to remove slow sweat drift using a 3 Hz high-pass filter, they eradicate the entire biological Delta band ($0.5\text{–}4\text{ Hz}$). Deep Non-REM sleep (Slow Wave Sleep) is defined almost entirely by high-amplitude Delta oscillations. A sleep-stage classifier trained on this data will be unable to distinguish deep sleep from light sleep or wakefulness.

### 3. Cleaner $\ne$ Better
> [!IMPORTANT]
> A visually pristine, perfectly smooth sine wave usually signifies **over-filtering**. Real neurobiological recordings are stochastic, turbulent, and structurally complex. Filtering blindly to make graphs look visually clean deletes real biological variance and creates artificial filter ringing (Gibbs phenomenon) around sharp transient events.

### 4. Offline vs. Real-Time Streaming Constraints
SciPy's `filtfilt` requires access to the entire recording in forward and reverse directions; it is strictly **non-causal** and cannot be deployed in real-time Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCIs). Real-time streaming applications must use causal filters (`scipy.signal.lfilter` or IIR/FIR with circular buffers), which introduce physical phase delays that must be calibrated.

## 6. Day 3 Knowledge Checkpoint & Worked Solutions

### Scenario A: Heavy Sweating & 0.2 Hz Baseline Drift
> *A patient is sweating heavily, causing electrode impedance to fluctuate slowly. The entire EEG recording is drifting up and down on a massive 0.2 Hz wave. Which specific filter (and approximate cutoff frequency) should conceptually fix this without damaging typical neural oscillations?*

**Authoritative Solution:**
- **Recommended Filter:** **High-Pass Filter.**
- **Cutoff Frequency ($f_c$):** **$0.5\text{ Hz}$** (or $0.1\text{–}0.5\text{ Hz}$).
- **Scientific Rationale:** A high-pass filter attenuates all spectral components below $f_c$ while passing frequencies above it. With $f_c = 0.5\text{ Hz}$, the $0.2\text{ Hz}$ sweat potential falls deep in the stopband ($0.2\text{ Hz} < 0.5\text{ Hz}$) and is strongly attenuated. Meanwhile, standard neurobiological rhythms (Delta starting at $0.5\text{–}1.0\text{ Hz}$, Theta, Alpha, Beta) remain in the passband.

### Scenario B: European Hospital 50 Hz Interference
> *You are analyzing an open-source dataset recorded in a European hospital. You visualize the raw time series and see a relentless, thick fuzz hiding the brain waves. What frequency is likely causing this, and what specific filter is designed to remove it?*

**Authoritative Solution:**
- **Identified Frequency:** **$50\text{ Hz}$ Mains Power-Line Interference.** (European power grids operate at 50 Hz).
- **Designed Filter:** **$50\text{ Hz}$ Notch Filter (Narrow Band-Stop Filter).**
- **Scientific Rationale:** Rather than applying a broad low-pass filter at $45\text{ Hz}$ (which would permanently eradicate Gamma activity from $45\text{ to }80\text{ Hz}$), a dedicated Notch filter (e.g., $49\text{–}51\text{ Hz}$) surgically suppresses the narrow $50\text{ Hz}$ spike while preserving genuine neural frequencies immediately above and below it.

### Scenario C: Deep Sleep Delta & The 3 Hz High-Pass Catastrophe
> *A junior researcher wants to study very slow Delta waves (0.5 to 4 Hz) to predict deep sleep stages. To "clean up the baseline," they apply a standard High-pass filter with a cutoff of 3 Hz. Why is this a catastrophic mistake?*

**Authoritative Solution:**
- **Catastrophic Failure:** The biological Delta band occupies $0.5\text{–}4.0\text{ Hz}$. A high-pass filter with $f_c = 3.0\text{ Hz}$ attenuates all frequencies below $3\text{ Hz}$.
- **Physiological Impact:** Frequencies from $0.5\text{ Hz}$ to $3.0\text{ Hz}$ comprise the vast majority of Slow Wave Sleep (SWS) spectral power. The researcher has physically erased the primary physiological biomarker for deep sleep before the machine learning model ever encounters the data. The classifier's apparent inability to detect deep sleep will not be an algorithmic failure, but an irreversible preprocessing error.

### Scenario D: Seizure Detection & The 40 Hz Low-Pass Catastrophe
> *You are building an AI to detect extremely rapid, high-frequency neural spikes that occur around 70 Hz just before a seizure. Your teammate runs the data through a Low-pass filter with a 40 Hz cutoff because "it makes the graphs look much cleaner." Explain exactly what your teammate just did to your machine learning project.*

**Authoritative Solution:**
- **Scientific Impact:** The teammate has destroyed the project's predictive target.
- **Physical Reason:** High-Frequency Oscillations (HFOs / fast ripples at $70\text{ Hz}$) sit far above the low-pass cutoff ($70\text{ Hz} > 40\text{ Hz}$) and will be attenuated into baseline noise.
- **The Core Research Lesson:** Visually smooth data is not equivalent to scientifically valid data. Optimizing aesthetic appearance at the expense of task-relevant frequency information eliminates the discriminative signal.
