# W4D2: Convolutional Neural Networks — Learned Local EEG Features and Spatiotemporal Filtering

## 1. Executive Briefing

Yesterday ([[w4d1|W4D1]]), we demonstrated that dense multi-layer perceptrons trained on handcrafted summary features easily overfit small cohorts because they lack structural awareness of temporal continuity. Today, we address this fundamental flaw by formulating **Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)** designed specifically for raw multichannel electroencephalography.

Electrophysiological signals possess strong local temporal correlation: neurophysiological events—such as sensorimotor mu desynchronization, beta rebound, or epileptic spike-wave complexes—occur as localized oscillatory bursts across short time horizons (50–300 ms). Rather than manually designing band-pass filters (Week 2), 1D convolutional layers slide learnable weight kernels across continuous voltage traces, automatically optimizing finite impulse response (FIR) filter banks directly from raw microvolts.

Today's module formalizes:
1. **The Mathematics of 1D Temporal Convolution:** Kernel size, stride, padding, weight sharing, and temporal receptive fields.
2. **Feature Maps and Pooling:** How max pooling acts as a non-linear oscillatory envelope detector while downsampling effective sampling rates.
3. **The Core Paradigm:** Replacing manual feature extraction with the end-to-end framework:
   $$\text{Multichannel EEG } [B, C, T] \xrightarrow{\text{CNN}} \text{Learned Feature Maps } [B, F] \xrightarrow{\text{Linear Head}} \text{Predictions } [B, K]$$
4. **Architectural Trace on Real EEG:** Executing `CompactEEGNet1D` on real 3-channel, 480-sample motor imagery epochs ($C_3, C_z, C_4$ from PhysioNet `S001`).

## 2. Mathematical Formulations of 1D Convolution for EEG

### 2.1 The 1D Discrete Convolution Operator

Let $x \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{in} \times T}$ represent a multichannel EEG epoch, where $C_{in}$ is the number of sensor electrodes and $T$ is the number of temporal sampling points. A 1D temporal [[convolution]] layer with $C_{out}$ filters applies learnable kernels $W \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{out} \times C_{in} \times K}$ and bias vectors $b \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{out}}$, where $K$ denotes the temporal kernel length.

For the $m$-th output feature map at time step $t$:
$$y_m[t] = \sum_{c=1}^{C_{in}} \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} x_c[t \cdot s + k] \cdot W_{m, c}[k] + b_m$$
where:
- $s$ is the **stride** (sample step size between successive kernel applications).
- $K$ is the **kernel size** in samples.
- The temporal duration of the kernel's receptive field in physical time is:
  $$\tau_{\text{kernel}} = \frac{K}{f_s} \text{ seconds}$$
  At sampling frequency $f_s = 160\text{ Hz}$, a kernel of length $K = 32$ spans exactly $\frac{32}{160} = 0.200\text{ s} = 200\text{ ms}$, precisely matching the timescale of two complete cycles of a 10 Hz sensorimotor $\mu$-rhythm.

### 2.2 Inductive Biases: Local Connectivity and Weight Sharing

Standard fully connected layers assign an independent weight to every sample in an epoch, resulting in massive parameter counts and zero spatial or temporal awareness. Convolutional architectures incorporate two decisive biological and physical inductive biases:
1. **Local Connectivity:** Each output unit computes activations exclusively from a localized temporal window of length $K$. Distant points cannot interact at Layer 1, respecting the localized nature of synaptic transients.
2. **Weight Sharing (Translation Invariance):** The identical kernel weights $W_{m, c}$ slide across the entire temporal duration. If a 10 Hz desynchronization burst occurs at $t = 1.0\text{ s}$ or $t = 2.2\text{ s}$, the exact same filter detects it. This translation equivariance dramatically reduces trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(C \cdot T \cdot H)$ to $\mathcal{O}(C_{in} \cdot C_{out} \cdot K)$.

### 2.3 Pooling Operations: Envelope Detection and Downsampling

Convolutional feature maps retain the original sampling resolution ($f_s = 160\text{ Hz}$). To extract low-frequency power envelopes and downsample sequence lengths, [[pooling]] operators are applied along the temporal dimension:
- **Max Pooling:** Computes the maximum value within a sliding window of length $P$ with stride $s_p$:
  $$p_m[t] = \max_{j \in [0, P-1]} y_m[t \cdot s_p + j]$$
  In electrophysiology, max pooling operates as an **envelope detector**: because high-frequency oscillations oscillate symmetrically above and below zero, taking the local maximum tracks the peak amplitude boundary (similar to full-wave rectification followed by peak extraction).
- **Temporal Downsampling:** A pooling stride of $s_p = 4$ reduces an input of $T = 480$ samples down to $T' = 120$ samples, scaling down the effective sampling rate from 160 Hz to $40\text{ Hz}$ while introducing local phase invariance.

## 3. The End-to-End Decoding Paradigm: `EEG -> CNN -> Features -> Prediction`

The fundamental architecture of modern neurotechnology replaces disconnected preprocessing pipelines with an integrated computational graph:

```text
========================================================================================================
                                 END-TO-END DECODING GRAPH
========================================================================================================
[Raw / Bandpassed EEG]    --> Shape: [Batch, Channels=3, Time=480]   (FS = 160 Hz, 3.0 s)
          │
          ▼  Layer 1: Temporal Conv1D (k=32, 200 ms) + BatchNorm1D + ReLU
[Temporal Feature Maps]  --> Shape: [Batch, Filters=8, Time=481]     (8 Learned Filter Banks)
          │
          ▼  Layer 2: Temporal MaxPool1D (k=4, stride=4)
[Oscillatory Envelopes]  --> Shape: [Batch, Filters=8, Time=120]     (Effective FS = 40 Hz)
          │
          ▼  Layer 3: Higher-Order Conv1D (k=16 pooled positions, 593.75 ms input receptive field) + BatchNorm1D + ReLU
[Latent Representations] --> Shape: [Batch, Filters=16, Time=121]    (Cross-Feature Interactions)
          │
          ▼  Layer 4: Adaptive Average Pooling (AdaptiveAvgPool1d(1))
[Trial Embedding Vector] --> Shape: [Batch, Features=16]             (Condensed Latent Code)
          │
          ▼  Layer 5: Linear Classification Head (Linear(16, 2))
[Predicted Logits]       --> Shape: [Batch, Classes=2]               (0 = LEFT_HAND, 1 = RIGHT_HAND)
========================================================================================================
```

## 4. Laboratory: Executing `CompactEEGNet1D` on Real Multichannel EEG

We implement `CompactEEGNet1D` in PyTorch, instantiating an architecture with exactly 2,922 trainable parameters. We trace tensor transformations and analyze the learned filter bank.

```python
import numpy as np
import torch
from generate_week4_plots import CompactEEGNet1D

# 1. Load the S01 validation-selected model and its training-run scaler.
checkpoint = torch.load('data/week4_cnn_checkpoint.pt', weights_only=True)
model = CompactEEGNet1D()
model.load_state_dict(checkpoint['model_state_dict'])
model.eval()
raw = np.load('data/week4_epochs.npy')[:1]  # uV, [1, 3, 480]
mean = np.array(checkpoint['channel_mean'], dtype=np.float32)
std = np.array(checkpoint['channel_std'], dtype=np.float32)
x = torch.from_numpy((raw-mean)/std)

# 2. Freeze batch-normalization statistics and inspect true tensor shapes.
with torch.no_grad():
    a = model.relu(model.bn1(model.conv1(x)))
    p = model.pool1(a)
    b = model.relu(model.bn2(model.conv2(p)))
    pooled = model.pool2(b).squeeze(-1)
    logits = model.classifier(pooled)
for name, value in [('Input', x), ('Conv1 + BN + ReLU', a),
                    ('MaxPool', p), ('Conv2 + BN + ReLU', b),
                    ('MeanPool + squeeze', pooled), ('Logits', logits)]:
    print(f'{name}: {list(value.shape)}')
print(f'Parameters: {sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters())}')
print(f'Selected S01 epoch: {checkpoint["best_epoch"]}')
```

### Parameter Count Breakdown
1. `conv1`: $(3 \times 32 \times 8) + 8 = 776$ weights
2. `bn1`: $8 \times 2 = 16$ weights ($\gamma, \beta$)
3. `conv2`: $(8 \times 16 \times 16) + 16 = 2,064$ weights
4. `bn2`: $16 \times 2 = 32$ weights
5. `classifier`: $(16 \times 2) + 2 = 34$ weights
**Sum:** $776 + 16 + 2,064 + 32 + 34 = 2,922$ parameters.

## 6. Daily Knowledge Checkpoint

### Question 1: Receptive Field Duration Calculation
**Prompt:** An investigator records EEG at $f_s = 250\text{ Hz}$ and configures a 1D temporal convolution layer with kernel size $K = 50$ samples, followed by a max pooling layer with kernel size $P = 5$ and stride $s = 5$.
1. Calculate the physical time duration of the temporal convolution kernel in milliseconds.
2. What is the effective sampling rate of the feature maps after the max pooling layer?
3. Calculate the Nyquist frequency of the signals entering subsequent layers.

**Solution:**
1. The kernel duration is $\tau = \frac{K}{f_s} = \frac{50}{250} = 0.200\text{ s} = 200\text{ ms}$.
2. With pooling stride $s = 5$, the effective temporal sampling rate is reduced by a factor of 5:
   $$f_{s,\text{eff}} = \frac{f_s}{s} = \frac{250}{5} = 50\text{ Hz}$$
3. The Nyquist frequency after pooling is half the effective sampling rate:
   $$f_{\text{Nyquist}} = \frac{f_{s,\text{eff}}}{2} = \frac{50}{2} = 25\text{ Hz}$$
   Any oscillatory activity above 25 Hz must be attenuated before or during pooling to prevent aliasing distortion in subsequent network layers.

### Question 2: Mathematical Output Dimension Formula
**Prompt:** Given an input tensor of temporal length $T_{in} = 480$ samples, a convolution layer with kernel size $K = 32$, stride $s = 1$, and zero-padding $p = 16$.
1. State the general mathematical formula for the output length $T_{out}$.
2. Compute the exact numerical output dimension $T_{out}$.
3. Explain why even kernel lengths ($K = 32$) with symmetric padding ($p = 16$) produce $T_{out} = 481$ rather than $480$.

**Solution:**
1. The output length for 1D convolution is given by:
   $$T_{out} = \left\lfloor \frac{T_{in} + 2p - K}{s} \right\rfloor + 1$$
2. Substituting the numerical parameters:
   $$T_{out} = \left\lfloor \frac{480 + 2(16) - 32}{1} \right\rfloor + 1 = \left\lfloor \frac{480 + 32 - 32}{1} \right\rfloor + 1 = 480 + 1 = 481$$
3. Symmetric padding adds $p = 16$ zeros to the left and $p = 16$ zeros to the right, increasing total input length to $480 + 32 = 512$. Because the kernel length $K = 32$ is even, centering the kernel requires an asymmetric split (15 on left, 16 on right). Symmetric padding by 16 on both sides introduces an extra boundary step, yielding 481 output samples. To maintain exact input length with even kernels, one can use PyTorch's `padding='same'` or slice the output tensor.

### Question 3: Inductive Bias Comparison
**Prompt:** Contrast the inductive biases of a dense fully connected layer ($\text{Linear}(1440 \to 16)$) and a 1D temporal convolution layer ($\text{Conv1d}(3 \to 8, K=32)$) when processing 3-channel, 480-sample EEG epochs. Explain why convolution is structurally superior for electrophysiology.

**Solution:**
- **Dense Linear Layer:** Assigns $1,440 \times 16 = 23,040$ independent weights. It possesses no inductive bias regarding time or space: shuffling the order of temporal samples or swapping electrodes produces an identical parameter budget and loss surface. It assumes all samples are equally independent and potentially interconnected, making it highly prone to memorizing spurious sample-specific noise.
- **1D Temporal Convolution:** Uses $3 \times 32 \times 8 = 768$ weights (a $30\times$ parameter reduction). It imposes two strict physical inductive biases:
  1. *Temporal Locality:* Neuronal population activity is temporally continuous; samples 10 ms apart are highly correlated, whereas samples 2 seconds apart are not locally phase-coupled.
  2. *Shift Invariance:* A characteristic neurophysiological transient (e.g., an evoked potential or oscillatory spindle) maintains its morphological identity regardless of whether it begins at 1.0 s or 1.5 s post-cue.
Because convolutional architectures embody the physical properties of continuous brain oscillations, they constrain optimization to neurophysiologically plausible functions.
