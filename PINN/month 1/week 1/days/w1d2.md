## 1. Day 2 Overview & Daily Goals

In physical systems, static values are rarely the primary quantities of interest; conservation laws (mass, momentum, energy) are fundamentally relations between **rates of change**. A gradient indicates a heat flux or pressure differential; a second derivative indicates curvature or diffusion.

The primary goals of Day 2 are:
1. Formalize the difference quotient and limit definition of single-variable derivatives.
2. Extend differentiation to multivariate coordinate spaces via partial derivatives.
3. Formulate the gradient vector $\nabla f$ and prove why it defines the direction of steepest ascent.
4. Derive the multivariable chain rule, establishing the mathematical cornerstone of both coordinate transformation and deep learning backpropagation.

## 2. Diagnostic & Previous-Day Verification

### Diagnostic Questions & Authoritative Solutions

#### 1. Why is the derivative defined as a limit rather than simple division $\Delta y / \Delta x$?
- Direct division at a single point yields $\frac{0}{0}$, which is undefined. The limit $\lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x+\Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x}$ describes the asymptotic trend of the average rate of change across an open neighborhood shrinking to the point $x$.

#### 2. Given a scalar field $f(x, y) = x^2 y + \sin(x)$, compute the partial derivatives $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$.
- Treating $y$ as a constant:
  $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x y + \cos(x)$$
- Treating $x$ as a constant:
  $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2$$

#### 3. What is the physical distinction between a partial derivative $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}$ and a material derivative $\frac{Du}{Dt}$ in fluid dynamics?
- $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}$ is the **Eulerian** rate of change observed at a fixed coordinate location in space.
- $\frac{Du}{Dt} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla u$ is the **Lagrangian** material derivative tracking the total rate of change experienced by a fluid parcel advecting with velocity $\mathbf{v}$.

## 3. Core Theory: The Differential Operator Arsenal

### 3.1 The Limit Definition & Geometric Tangency
The derivative measures the instantaneous linear approximation of a function at point $x_0$:

$$f(x) \approx f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \mathcal{O}((x - x_0)^2)$$

Where:
$$f'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h}$$

If this limit exists, $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$, and the affine line $y = f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0)$ is the unique tangent line to the curve.

### 3.2 The Multivariate Gradient Vector Field
For a differentiable scalar function $\Phi: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$, the gradient $\nabla \Phi(\mathbf{x})$ packages all directional information:

$$\nabla \Phi(\mathbf{x}) = \left[ \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial x_1}, \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial x_2}, \dots, \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial x_d} \right]^T \in \mathbb{R}^d$$

![Gradient Vector Field](../assets/gradient_vector_field.png)

**Figure 1.** *Scalar potential surface $\Phi(x_1, x_2)$ overlaid with normalized gradient vectors $\nabla \Phi$ (white arrows). Notice that vectors are perpendicular to the contour level curves and point toward local maxima.*

#### Proof: Gradient Points in the Direction of Steepest Ascent
Let $\hat{\mathbf{v}}$ be an arbitrary unit vector ($\|\hat{\mathbf{v}}\|_2 = 1$). The directional derivative of $\Phi$ in direction $\hat{\mathbf{v}}$ is:

$$D_{\hat{\mathbf{v}}} \Phi(\mathbf{x}) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\Phi(\mathbf{x} + h\hat{\mathbf{v}}) - \Phi(\mathbf{x})}{h} = \nabla \Phi(\mathbf{x}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{v}}$$

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality / cosine formula:

$$\nabla \Phi(\mathbf{x}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{v}} = \|\nabla \Phi(\mathbf{x})\| \|\hat{\mathbf{v}}\| \cos \theta = \|\nabla \Phi(\mathbf{x})\| \cos \theta$$

Since $\cos \theta \le 1$, the directional derivative attains its maximum value $\|\nabla \Phi(\mathbf{x})\|$ if and only if $\cos \theta = 1$, which occurs when $\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \frac{\nabla \Phi(\mathbf{x})}{\|\nabla \Phi(\mathbf{x})\|}$. Thus, $\nabla \Phi$ points in the direction of steepest ascent, and $-\nabla \Phi$ points in the direction of steepest descent.

### 3.3 The Multivariable Chain Rule
Let $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}) \in \mathbb{R}^m$ and $f(\mathbf{z}) \in \mathbb{R}$. The derivative with respect to coordinate $x_j$ is:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j} = \sum_{k=1}^m \frac{\partial f}{\partial z_k} \frac{\partial z_k}{\partial x_j}$$

In PINNs, when a neural network layer computes $\mathbf{z}^{(l)} = \sigma(\mathbf{W}^{(l)} \mathbf{z}^{(l-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(l)})$, every derivative computed during backpropagation and coordinate differentiation is an algorithmic application of this exact formula.

## 4. Practical Laboratory: Numerical Gradient & Directional Derivative

### 4.1 Concept → Code → Output → Interpretation

#### 1. Concept
We compute the analytical and numerical gradient of $\Phi(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + 3 x_1 x_2 + 2 x_2^2$ at coordinate $\mathbf{x}_0 = (1.0, 2.0)^T$, and evaluate the directional derivative along the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, 1)^T$.

#### 2. Code
```python
import numpy as np

def phi(x):
    return x[0]**2 + 3.0 * x[0] * x[1] + 2.0 * x[1]**2

def grad_analytical(x):
    d_x1 = 2.0 * x[0] + 3.0 * x[1]
    d_x2 = 3.0 * x[0] + 4.0 * x[1]
    return np.array([d_x1, d_x2])

x0 = np.array([1.0, 2.0])
g_exact = grad_analytical(x0)

# Central difference gradient approximation
h = 1e-5
g_num = np.zeros(2)
for i in range(2):
    x_plus = x0.copy()
    x_minus = x0.copy()
    x_plus[i] += h
    x_minus[i] -= h
    g_num[i] = (phi(x_plus) - phi(x_minus)) / (2 * h)

# Directional derivative along v = [1, 1] / sqrt(2)
v = np.array([1.0, 1.0]) / np.sqrt(2.0)
dir_deriv_exact = np.dot(g_exact, v)
dir_deriv_num = (phi(x0 + h * v) - phi(x0 - h * v)) / (2 * h)

print(f"Point x0: {x0}")
print(f"Analytical Gradient: {g_exact}")
print(f"Numerical Gradient:  {g_num}")
print(f"Gradient L2 Norm (Steepest Slope): {np.linalg.norm(g_exact):.6f}")
print(f"Directional Derivative (Analytical): {dir_deriv_exact:.6f}")
print(f"Directional Derivative (Numerical):  {dir_deriv_num:.6f}")
```

#### 3. Output
```text
Point x0: [1. 2.]
Analytical Gradient: [ 8. 11.]
Numerical Gradient:  [ 8. 11.]
Gradient L2 Norm (Steepest Slope): 13.601471
Directional Derivative (Analytical): 13.435029
Directional Derivative (Numerical):  13.435029
```

#### 4. Interpretation
At coordinate $(1, 2)$, the analytical gradient evaluates to $[2(1) + 3(2), 3(1) + 4(2)] = [8, 11]$. The central difference approximation matches the analytical vector to within double-precision roundoff. The maximum possible instantaneous slope is the magnitude $\|\nabla \Phi\| = \sqrt{8^2 + 11^2} = \sqrt{185} \approx 13.601471$. Along the diagonal direction $\hat{\mathbf{v}}$, the slope is $\frac{8 + 11}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{19}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 13.435029$, confirming the inner product projection theorem.

## 5. Daily Knowledge Mastery Checkpoint

### Problem 1: Higher-Order Derivatives & The Hessian
**Question:** For scalar field $\Phi(x_1, x_2) = x_1^3 x_2^2$, compute the Hessian matrix $\mathbf{H}_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 \Phi}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}$ and state Schwarz's theorem regarding mixed partials.  
**Authoritative Solution:**  
First derivatives:
$$\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial x_1} = 3 x_1^2 x_2^2, \quad \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial x_2} = 2 x_1^3 x_2$$
Second derivatives:
$$\frac{\partial^2 \Phi}{\partial x_1^2} = 6 x_1 x_2^2, \quad \frac{\partial^2 \Phi}{\partial x_2^2} = 2 x_1^3$$
Mixed partials:
$$\frac{\partial^2 \Phi}{\partial x_1 \partial x_2} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1}(2 x_1^3 x_2) = 6 x_1^2 x_2, \quad \frac{\partial^2 \Phi}{\partial x_2 \partial x_1} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2}(3 x_1^2 x_2^2) = 6 x_1^2 x_2$$
Thus, the Hessian is symmetric:
$$\mathbf{H} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 x_1 x_2^2 & 6 x_1^2 x_2 \\ 6 x_1^2 x_2 & 2 x_1^3 \end{bmatrix}$$
Schwarz's (Clairaut's) theorem states that if mixed second partial derivatives are continuous in an open neighborhood, then $\frac{\partial^2 \Phi}{\partial x_i \partial x_j} = \frac{\partial^2 \Phi}{\partial x_j \partial x_i}$.

### Problem 2: Chain Rule with Hidden Intermediate Layers
**Question:** Let $u = \sin(w)$, where $w = x^2 + y^2$. Compute $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}$ and $\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$ using the chain rule.  
**Authoritative Solution:**  
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{du}{dw} \frac{\partial w}{\partial x} = \cos(w) \cdot (2x) = 2x \cos(x^2 + y^2)$$
For the second derivative, applying the product and chain rules:
$$\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}[2x \cos(w)] = 2 \cos(w) + 2x \left(-\sin(w) \frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\right) = 2 \cos(w) - 4x^2 \sin(w)$$
