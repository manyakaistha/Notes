## 1. Day 4 Overview & Daily Goals

In Day 3, we discretized continuous domains using Latin Hypercube Sampling to estimate the interior physics residual. However, minimizing the interior residual alone does not yield a well-posed solution; the model must simultaneously satisfy spatial boundary conditions, initial states, and empirical sensor observations. Standard PINN implementations bundle these competing requirements into a single composite objective functional using static penalty weights. In practice, this naïve combination frequently causes catastrophic training failure: the optimizer drives boundary loss to zero while the interior PDE residual stagnates in unphysical local minima.

The primary objective of Day 4 is to resolve **multi-objective loss pathologies and boundary constraint formulations**:
1. Validate prerequisite understandings of constrained optimization, Lagrange multipliers, and Pareto optimality through diagnostic checkpoints.
2. Formulate the general composite PINN loss functional $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}(\theta)$ and contrast soft penalty regularization against exact hard boundary constraints via distance-function ansatzes.
3. Mathematically dissect **Gradient Stiffness**: analyzing the spectral condition numbers of the constituent Hessian matrices ($\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{bc}}$ vs. $\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{pde}}$) and proving why gradient magnitudes diverge by up to $100\times$.
4. Derive the **Learning Rate Annealing** algorithm (Wang et al., 2021), establishing its dynamic gradient-tracking update equations.
5. Execute an empirical PyTorch laboratory implementing dynamic gradient tracking, demonstrating how adaptive weighting restores balanced optimization.

## 2. Prerequisite Diagnostic Checkpoints & Authoritative Solutions

Before implementing loss balancing, we must examine the optimization dynamics of competing loss functionals.

### Diagnostic Checkpoints & Authoritative Solutions

#### Checkpoint 1: Pareto Optimality vs. Scalarization
*Question:* A PINN objective is intrinsically a multi-objective optimization problem: $\min_\theta [\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}}(\theta), \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\theta), \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ic}}(\theta)]^T$. Define *Pareto optimality* in this context. Why does linear scalarization $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}} = \sum \lambda_i \mathcal{L}_i$ with fixed weights $\lambda_i$ often fail to discover physically acceptable Pareto optimal points?  
*Solution:* A parameter vector $\theta^*$ is *Pareto optimal* if there exists no other parameter vector $\theta \in \Theta$ that strictly decreases at least one loss objective $\mathcal{L}_j(\theta) < \mathcal{L}_j(\theta^*)$ without simultaneously increasing another objective $\mathcal{L}_k(\theta) > \mathcal{L}_k(\theta^*)$. The set of all Pareto optimal points forms the *Pareto frontier*. Linear scalarization converts the vector objective into a single scalar surrogate $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}(\theta) = \sum \lambda_i \mathcal{L}_i(\theta)$. If the Pareto frontier is non-convex (common in stiff, non-linear PDE landscapes such as Navier-Stokes or high-frequency Helmholtz systems), linear scalarization is mathematically incapable of identifying points located inside the non-convex indentations of the frontier, regardless of the choice of static weights $\lambda_i$. Fixed multipliers force the gradient trajectory along rigid linear hyperplanes, resulting in optimization trapping.

#### Checkpoint 2: The Physical Penalty of Soft Boundary Constraints
*Question:* In a soft-constraint formulation, boundary conditions are enforced as penalties: $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}} = \frac{1}{N_b}\sum_{i=1}^{N_b} |u_\theta(\mathbf{x}_b^i) - g(\mathbf{x}_b^i)|^2$. Why can soft constraints never guarantee exact physical boundary compliance, and what physical consequences arise in fluid dynamics if boundary conditions are violated by even $0.1\%$?  
*Solution:* In unconstrained gradient optimization, penalty methods achieve exact constraint satisfaction ($u_\theta(\mathbf{x}_b) = g(\mathbf{x}_b)$) only in the asymptotic limit as penalty multiplier $\lambda_{\mathrm{bc}} \to \infty$. For any finite penalty weight $\lambda_{\mathrm{bc}} < \infty$, the global minimum of the composite loss represents a compromise where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}} > 0$. In fluid dynamics governed by the Navier-Stokes equations, violating the no-slip Dirichlet boundary condition ($\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$ at solid walls) by even $0.1\%$ permits artificial fluid slip and unphysical mass flux across solid boundaries. This disrupts the boundary layer shear stress $\tau_w = \mu \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}$, corrupts the boundary vorticity generation mechanism, and causes completely erroneous downstream vortex shedding frequencies and drag coefficients.

#### Checkpoint 3: Exact Hard Boundary Constraint Formulation (Ansatz Method)
*Question:* Formulate an architectural transformation that guarantees exact satisfaction of homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions $u(\mathbf{x}) = 0, \forall \mathbf{x} \in \partial \Omega$, completely eliminating $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}}$ from the loss function.  
*Solution:* Exact hard boundary satisfaction is achieved by defining a modified network ansatz using a smooth *distance function* $D(\mathbf{x})$:
$$u_\theta(\mathbf{x}) \equiv D(\mathbf{x}) \cdot \mathcal{N}_\theta(\mathbf{x})$$
where $\mathcal{N}_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ is an unconstrained neural network and $D: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ is a known, smooth function satisfying:
$$D(\mathbf{x}) = 0, \quad \forall \mathbf{x} \in \partial \Omega \quad \text{and} \quad D(\mathbf{x}) > 0, \quad \forall \mathbf{x} \in \Omega^{\circ}$$
For non-homogeneous Dirichlet conditions $u(\mathbf{x}) = g(\mathbf{x})$ on $\partial \Omega$, the ansatz is generalized to:
$$u_\theta(\mathbf{x}) \equiv g_{\mathrm{ext}}(\mathbf{x}) + D(\mathbf{x}) \cdot \mathcal{N}_\theta(\mathbf{x})$$
where $g_{\mathrm{ext}}(\mathbf{x})$ is a smooth extension of the boundary values into the interior. For any choice of network weights $\theta$, $u_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = g(\mathbf{x}) + 0 \cdot \mathcal{N}_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = g(\mathbf{x})$ on $\partial \Omega$ identically. The boundary loss term is entirely eliminated ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}} \equiv 0$).

#### Checkpoint 4: The Mathematical Origin of Gradient Stiffness
*Question:* Mathematically explain why the parameter gradient norm of the boundary loss $\|\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}}\|$ is often $10^2$ to $10^4$ times larger than the interior PDE residual gradient norm $\|\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}}\|$ in early training.  
*Solution:* The boundary loss is a direct zero-order mean squared error on the output layer: $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}} = \frac{1}{N_b}\sum (u_\theta - g)^2$. The gradient with respect to output weights $W^{(L)}$ is simply $\nabla_{W^{(L)}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}} = \frac{2}{N_b}\sum (u_\theta - g) z^{(L-1)}$, which has direct, unhindered backpropagation path length $\mathcal{O}(1)$. In contrast, the PDE residual loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}} = \frac{1}{N_f}\sum |\mathcal{N}[u_\theta] - f|^2$ involves second-order coordinate derivatives $\partial_{xx} u_\theta$. As derived in Day 2, differentiating twice with respect to input coordinates introduces products of internal weight matrices and activation second derivatives: $\nabla_{W^{(L)}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}} \sim \frac{2}{N_f}\sum \mathcal{R} \cdot (W^{(1)})^2 \cdot \sigma''(z)$. If weights or coordinate scales are non-unit, or if activations saturate, the backpropagated sensitivities through the secondary graph become severely ill-scaled. The boundary loss landscape possesses steep, highly curved parabolic valleys, whereas the interior PDE residual landscape is relatively flat, creating extreme Hessian eigenvalue disparity.

#### Checkpoint 5: Instability of Ad-Hoc Weight Tuning
*Question:* Why does manual heuristic tuning of loss multipliers (e.g., arbitrarily setting $\lambda_{\mathrm{bc}} = 100$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{pde}} = 1$) fail when training across different physical regimes (e.g., varying the Reynolds number in fluid flow)?  
*Solution:* Physical systems undergo bifurcations and regime shifts as dimensionless parameters vary. For example, in the Navier-Stokes equations, increasing the Reynolds number $\mathrm{Re} = \frac{U L}{\nu}$ from $10$ to $1000$ contracts the boundary layer thickness from $\delta \sim \frac{L}{\sqrt{10}}$ down to $\delta \sim \frac{L}{\sqrt{1000}} \approx 0.03 L$. The interior convective non-linearity $\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla \mathbf{u}$ becomes dominant, altering the condition number of the differential operator by orders of magnitude over the course of training. A static multiplier configured for $\mathrm{Re} = 10$ will either completely fail to enforce the thin boundary layer or cause the PDE residual to be overwhelmed by boundary penalties. Static weights cannot adapt to the dynamic re-scaling of gradients that occurs as the neural network transitions from coarse macroscopic fitting to fine boundary layer resolution.

## 3. Core Technical Exposition

### 3.1 Composite Loss Formulations & Boundary Enforcement

The standard forward PINN seeks network parameters $\theta^*$ that minimize a composite multi-objective functional:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}(\theta) = \lambda_f \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}}(\theta) + \lambda_b \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\theta) + \lambda_0 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ic}}(\theta) + \lambda_d \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}}(\theta)$$

where the individual mean squared error components are:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N_f} \sum_{i=1}^{N_f} \left\| \mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{x}, t}[u_\theta(\mathbf{x}_f^i, t_f^i)] - f(\mathbf{x}_f^i, t_f^i) \right\|_2^2$$

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N_b} \sum_{i=1}^{N_b} \left\| \mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{x}}[u_\theta(\mathbf{x}_b^i, t_b^i)] - g(\mathbf{x}_b^i, t_b^i) \right\|_2^2$$

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ic}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N_0} \sum_{i=1}^{N_0} \left\| u_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0^i, 0) - u_0(\mathbf{x}_0^i) \right\|_2^2$$

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N_d} \sum_{i=1}^{N_d} \left\| u_\theta(\mathbf{x}_d^i, t_d^i) - u_{\mathrm{meas}}^i \right\|_2^2$$

### 3.2 Gradient Stiffness: Spectral Anatomy of the Loss Landscape

The fundamental challenge in optimizing composite PINN losses is **Gradient Stiffness** (Wang, Teng, & Perdikaris, 2021).

![Gradient Stiffness and Pathology](../assets/gradient_stiffness_and_pathology.png)

**Figure 7.** Mathematical pathology of Gradient Stiffness in multi-objective PINN optimization. (Left) The composite loss landscape forms an ill-conditioned, elongated anisotropic valley. (Right) Layer-wise gradient norm magnitude distribution across network depth. Boundary condition gradients $\|\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}}\|$ overpower interior PDE residual gradients $\|\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}}\|$ by over two orders of magnitude, causing the optimizer to satisfy boundary values prematurely while freezing the interior solution in an unphysical state.

Mathematically, let $\mathbf{H}_k = \nabla_\theta^2 \mathcal{L}_k(\theta)$ be the Hessian matrix of loss component $k$. The total Hessian is:
$$\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{total}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{pde}} \mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{pde}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{bc}} \mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{bc}}$$
Due to the coordinate derivatives present in $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}}$, the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{pde}}$ and $\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{bc}}$ span vastly different spectral ranges:
$$\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{bc}}) \gg \lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{pde}})$$
The condition number of the unweighted composite Hessian approaches:
$$\kappa(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{total}}) = \frac{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{total}})}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{total}})} \sim 10^6 - 10^8$$
In first-order gradient descent with step size $\eta$, convergence along the $i$-th eigenvector direction requires $\eta < \frac{2}{\lambda_i}$. To avoid numerical instability along the stiff boundary direction ($\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{bc}})$), the learning rate must be throttled to $\eta \le \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{bc}})}$. However, with this tiny step size, progress along the interior PDE direction is governed by the contraction rate $\left(1 - \eta \lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{pde}})\right) \approx \left(1 - \frac{2\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{pde}})}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{bc}})}\right) \approx 1$. Interior physics progress completely stagnates.

### 3.3 Learning Rate Annealing Algorithm

To eliminate gradient stiffness, Wang et al. (2021) introduced the **Learning Rate Annealing** algorithm. Rather than maintaining fixed penalty weights, the algorithm dynamically rescales the loss weights at every iteration (or every $K$ iterations) so that the backpropagated parameter gradient norms from boundary and data constraints match the maximum gradient norm of the interior PDE residual.

#### Algorithmic Definition:

1. At training iteration $n$, compute the backpropagated parameter gradients for each loss component independently:
   $$\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{pde}} = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}}(\theta^{(n)})$$
   $$\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{bc}} = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}}(\theta^{(n)})$$
   $$\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{ic}} = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ic}}(\theta^{(n)})$$

2. Compute the target scaling factor $\hat{\lambda}_k$ for each constraint $k \in \{\mathrm{bc}, \mathrm{ic}\}$:
   $$\hat{\lambda}_k = \frac{\max_{\theta} \left| \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}}(\theta^{(n)}) \right|}{\overline{\left| \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_k(\theta^{(n)}) \right|}}$$
   where $\max_\theta |\mathbf{g}|$ is the maximum absolute gradient component across all network parameters, and $\overline{|\mathbf{g}|}$ is the mean absolute gradient component.

3. Update the operational loss weights using an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) with momentum $\alpha \in [0.1, 0.9]$:
   $$\lambda_k^{(n+1)} = (1 - \alpha) \lambda_k^{(n)} + \alpha \hat{\lambda}_k$$

![Learning Rate Annealing Convergence](../assets/learning_rate_annealing_convergence.png)

**Figure 8.** Optimization trajectory benchmark: Static Multi-Objective Weighting vs. Dynamic Learning Rate Annealing. Under static weighting (red), the boundary loss drops rapidly, but the PDE residual stagnates at an asymptotic plateau ($\sim 10^{-2}$). Under dynamic Learning Rate Annealing (green), loss weights continuously adapt, maintaining balanced gradient flow that drives the interior physics residual down to machine precision ($\le 10^{-6}$).

## 4. Runnable Laboratory: Dynamic Gradient Tracking & Annealing

The following self-contained script evaluates the 1D Poisson boundary value problem:
$$-\frac{d^2 u}{dx^2} = \pi^2 \sin(\pi x), \quad x \in [0, 1], \quad u(0) = 0, \quad u(1) = 0$$
Analytical solution: $u(x) = \sin(\pi x)$.

The script inspects the gradient norms of the PDE residual and boundary conditions, calculates the target annealed multiplier $\hat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{bc}}$, and demonstrates the dynamic rebalancing mechanism.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import numpy as np

# Set deterministic seed
torch.manual_seed(42)

print("=== PINN LAB W3D4: GRADIENT STIFFNESS & LEARNING RATE ANNEALING ===")

# 1. Instantiate 3-layer MLP surrogate
model = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Linear(1, 32),
    nn.Tanh(),
    nn.Linear(32, 32),
    nn.Tanh(),
    nn.Linear(32, 1)
)

# 2. Spatiotemporal Coordinates
x_colloc = torch.linspace(0.0, 1.0, 100)[:, None].requires_grad_(True)
x_bc = torch.tensor([[0.0], [1.0]], dtype=torch.float32, requires_grad=True)

# 3. Compute PDE Residual Loss: -u_xx - pi^2 sin(pi*x) = 0
u_c = model(x_colloc)
u_x = torch.autograd.grad(u_c, x_colloc, torch.ones_like(u_c), create_graph=True)[0]
u_xx = torch.autograd.grad(u_x, x_colloc, torch.ones_like(u_x), create_graph=True)[0]
f_source = (np.pi**2) * torch.sin(np.pi * x_colloc)
loss_pde = torch.mean((-u_xx - f_source)**2)

# 4. Compute Boundary Loss: u(0)^2 + u(1)^2
u_b = model(x_bc)
loss_bc = torch.mean(u_b**2)

# 5. Evaluate Component Gradient Vectors with allow_unused=True
# (Output layer bias has zero second derivative in linear PDEs)
grad_pde = torch.autograd.grad(loss_pde, model.parameters(), retain_graph=True, allow_unused=True)
grad_bc = torch.autograd.grad(loss_bc, model.parameters(), retain_graph=True, allow_unused=True)

grad_pde = [g if g is not None else torch.zeros_like(p) for g, p in zip(grad_pde, model.parameters())]
grad_bc = [g if g is not None else torch.zeros_like(p) for g, p in zip(grad_bc, model.parameters())]

# 6. Quantitative Norm and Annealing Computation
norm_pde = torch.sqrt(sum(torch.sum(g**2) for g in grad_pde)).item()
norm_bc = torch.sqrt(sum(torch.sum(g**2) for g in grad_bc)).item()

max_pde = max(torch.max(torch.abs(g)).item() for g in grad_pde)
mean_bc = np.mean([torch.mean(torch.abs(g)).item() for g in grad_bc])

# Target Annealed Weight
lambda_hat_bc = max_pde / max(mean_bc, 1e-8)
alpha = 0.9
lambda_bc_init = 1.0
lambda_bc_updated = (1.0 - alpha) * lambda_bc_init + alpha * lambda_hat_bc

stiffness_ratio = norm_pde / max(norm_bc, 1e-8)

print(f"PDE Residual Gradient Norm:  ||grad_theta L_pde|| = {norm_pde:.4f}")
print(f"Boundary Penalty Grad Norm:  ||grad_theta L_bc||  = {norm_bc:.4f}")
print(f"Gradient Stiffness Ratio:    ||grad_pde|| / ||grad_bc|| = {stiffness_ratio:.2f}x")
print("-" * 75)
print(f"Max Absolute PDE Gradient:   max |grad_pde| = {max_pde:.6f}")
print(f"Mean Absolute BC Gradient:   mean |grad_bc| = {mean_bc:.6f}")
print(f"Target Annealed Multiplier:  lambda_bc_hat  = {lambda_hat_bc:.4f}")
print(f"Updated Annealed Weight:     lambda_bc      = {lambda_bc_updated:.4f}")
```

### Physical & Quantitative Interpretation

This dynamic magnification ensures that boundary gradients exert equal steering authority on network weights, pinning the boundary coordinates to zero while the interior residual relaxes.

## 5. Daily Mastery Checkpoints & Worked Quantitative Problems

### Mastery Checkpoint 1: Construction of Distance Functions for Complex Domains
*Problem:* Consider a 2D rectangular domain $\Omega = [0, L_x] \times [0, L_y]$ with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions $u(x, y) = 0$ on all four outer walls.  
1. Construct an analytical, smooth $C^\infty$ distance function $D(x, y)$ that satisfies $D(x, y) = 0$ on $\partial \Omega$ and $D(x, y) > 0$ in the interior $\Omega^\circ$.  
2. If non-homogeneous Dirichlet conditions are imposed: $u(0, y) = 0, u(L_x, y) = 0, u(x, 0) = 0$, but the top wall is driven at velocity $u(x, L_y) = U_0 \sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{L_x}\right)$, construct the complete hard constraint ansatz $u_\theta(x, y)$.

*Solution:*\
1. **Distance Function for Rectangle:**  
   The boundary consists of four lines: $x = 0, x = L_x, y = 0, y = L_y$.  
   A smooth distance function can be constructed by the product of linear boundary offsets:
   $$D(x, y) = x (L_x - x) y (L_y - y)$$
   - On $x = 0$ or $x = L_x$: $D(x, y) = 0$.  
   - On $y = 0$ or $y = L_y$: $D(x, y) = 0$.  
   - For all $(x, y) \in (0, L_x) \times (0, L_y)$: each term is strictly positive, so $D(x, y) > 0$.  
   - To make $D(x, y)$ scale with unit order of magnitude, it is typically normalized by the domain area: $D_{\mathrm{norm}}(x, y) = \frac{16}{L_x^2 L_y^2} x(L_x - x) y(L_y - y)$, such that $\max_{(x, y)} D_{\mathrm{norm}} = 1.0$.
2. **Hard Constraint Ansatz for Lid-Driven Cavity:**  
   We require a boundary extension function $g_{\mathrm{ext}}(x, y)$ that equals $U_0 \sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{L_x}\right)$ at $y = L_y$ and zero on the other three walls.  
   Let:
   $$g_{\mathrm{ext}}(x, y) = U_0 \left( \frac{y}{L_y} \right) \sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{L_x}\right)$$
   - At $y = L_y$: $g_{\mathrm{ext}}(x, L_y) = U_0 (1) \sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{L_x}\right)$, exactly matching the top wall.  
   - At $y = 0$: $g_{\mathrm{ext}}(x, 0) = 0$.  
   - At $x = 0$ and $x = L_x$: $\sin(0) = \sin(\pi) = 0$, so $g_{\mathrm{ext}} = 0$ on both side walls.  
   The full ansatz is:
   $$u_\theta(x, y) = U_0 \left( \frac{y}{L_y} \right) \sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{L_x}\right) + x(L_x - x) y(L_y - y) \cdot \mathcal{N}_\theta(x, y)$$
   For any neural network $\mathcal{N}_\theta$, this surrogate matches all four boundary conditions with zero boundary loss.

### Mastery Checkpoint 2: Hessian Spectral Radius and Learning Rate Bounds
*Problem:* In a linear PINN formulation, the PDE loss has Hessian $\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{pde}}$ with maximum eigenvalue $\lambda_{\max}^{\mathrm{pde}} = 2.5$, while the boundary loss has Hessian $\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{bc}}$ with maximum eigenvalue $\lambda_{\max}^{\mathrm{bc}} = 850.0$.  
1. Compute the maximum stable learning rate $\eta_{\max}^{\mathrm{static}}$ for gradient descent when using static multipliers $\lambda_{\mathrm{pde}} = 1.0, \lambda_{\mathrm{bc}} = 1.0$.  
2. Compute the resulting convergence rate factor $\rho_{\mathrm{pde}} = 1 - \eta \lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{pde}}$ for the interior PDE residual if $\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{pde}} = 0.05$.  
3. If Learning Rate Annealing rebalances weights such that $\lambda_{\mathrm{pde}} \lambda_{\max}^{\mathrm{pde}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{bc}} \lambda_{\max}^{\mathrm{bc}} = 100.0$, compute the new stable learning rate and the improved convergence contraction factor.

*Solution:*\
1. **Static Maximum Stable Learning Rate:**  
   The total Hessian is $\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{total}} = \mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{pde}} + \mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{bc}}$.  
   Its spectral radius is bounded by $\lambda_{\max}^{\mathrm{total}} \approx \lambda_{\max}^{\mathrm{pde}} + \lambda_{\max}^{\mathrm{bc}} = 2.5 + 850.0 = 852.5$.  
   Gradient descent stability requires:
   $$\eta < \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}^{\mathrm{total}}} = \frac{2}{852.5} \approx 0.002346$$
2. **Interior PDE Contraction Factor under Static Weighting:**  
   With $\eta = 0.002346$ and $\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{pde}} = 0.05$:
   $$\rho_{\mathrm{pde}} = 1 - \eta \lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{pde}} = 1 - (0.002346)(0.05) = 1 - 0.0001173 = 0.999883$$
   The interior residual decays by only $0.0117\%$ per iteration. Reaching a $1000\times$ error reduction requires $\ln(10^{-3}) / \ln(0.999883) \approx -6.908 / (-0.0001173) \approx 58,890\text{ iterations}$.
3. **Dynamic Annealing Rebalancing:**  
   Under rebalanced weights $\lambda_{\mathrm{pde}} = \frac{100.0}{2.5} = 40.0$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{bc}} = \frac{100.0}{850.0} \approx 0.1176$:  
   The rebalanced total Hessian has spectral radius $\lambda_{\max}^{\mathrm{rebalanced}} \approx 100.0 + 100.0 = 200.0$.  
   The new maximum stable learning rate is:
   $$\eta_{\mathrm{new}} < \frac{2}{200.0} = 0.0100$$
   The effective PDE eigenvalue is scaled by $\lambda_{\mathrm{pde}} = 40.0$, giving $\lambda_{\min, \text{eff}}^{\mathrm{pde}} = 40.0 \times 0.05 = 2.0$.  
   The new contraction factor is:
   $$\rho_{\mathrm{pde}}^{\mathrm{new}} = 1 - (0.0100)(2.0) = 1 - 0.0200 = 0.9800$$
   Iterations required for $1000\times$ reduction: $\ln(10^{-3}) / \ln(0.9800) \approx -6.908 / (-0.0202) \approx 342\text{ iterations}$.  
   Learning Rate Annealing achieves a **$172\times$ acceleration** in convergence speed.

### Mastery Checkpoint 3: Pareto Conflicting Gradients (PCGrad) Mechanics
*Problem:* In multi-objective optimization, two loss components $\mathcal{L}_1$ and $\mathcal{L}_2$ produce gradient vectors $\mathbf{g}_1 = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_1$ and $\mathbf{g}_2 = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_2$.  
1. State the mathematical condition for *gradient conflict*.  
2. Describe the Projecting Conflicting Gradients (PCGrad, Yu et al., 2020) surgery rule and write the formula for the modified gradient $\mathbf{g}_1^*$ when $\mathbf{g}_1 \cdot \mathbf{g}_2 < 0$. Prove that $\mathbf{g}_1^* \cdot \mathbf{g}_2 = 0$.

*Solution:*\
1. **Condition for Gradient Conflict:**  
   Two objective gradients conflict if and only if their inner product is strictly negative:
   $$\langle \mathbf{g}_1, \mathbf{g}_2 \rangle = \mathbf{g}_1 \cdot \mathbf{g}_2 = \|\mathbf{g}_1\| \|\mathbf{g}_2\| \cos(\phi) < 0$$
   which occurs when the angle between them exceeds $\pi/2$ ($\phi > 90^\circ$). In this regime, taking a step along $-\mathbf{g}_1$ strictly increases $\mathcal{L}_2$, creating destructive interference where updates for one loss degrade the other.
2. **PCGrad Surgery Rule:**  
   If $\mathbf{g}_1 \cdot \mathbf{g}_2 < 0$, PCGrad projects $\mathbf{g}_1$ onto the normal plane of $\mathbf{g}_2$, removing the component that conflicts with $\mathbf{g}_2$:
   $$\mathbf{g}_1^* = \mathbf{g}_1 - \frac{\mathbf{g}_1 \cdot \mathbf{g}_2}{\|\mathbf{g}_2\|^2} \mathbf{g}_2$$
   If $\mathbf{g}_1 \cdot \mathbf{g}_2 \ge 0$, no projection is applied: $\mathbf{g}_1^* = \mathbf{g}_1$.  
   *Proof of Orthogonality:*
   $$\mathbf{g}_1^* \cdot \mathbf{g}_2 = \left( \mathbf{g}_1 - \frac{\mathbf{g}_1 \cdot \mathbf{g}_2}{\|\mathbf{g}_2\|^2} \mathbf{g}_2 \right) \cdot \mathbf{g}_2 = \mathbf{g}_1 \cdot \mathbf{g}_2 - \frac{\mathbf{g}_1 \cdot \mathbf{g}_2}{\|\mathbf{g}_2\|^2} (\mathbf{g}_2 \cdot \mathbf{g}_2) = \mathbf{g}_1 \cdot \mathbf{g}_2 - (\mathbf{g}_1 \cdot \mathbf{g}_2) = 0$$
   The modified gradient $\mathbf{g}_1^*$ is strictly orthogonal to $\mathbf{g}_2$, completely eliminating destructive interference.
