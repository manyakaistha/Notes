## 2. Prerequisite Diagnostic Checkpoints & Authoritative Solutions

Before combining Adam with L-BFGS, we must examine the convergence mechanics of first- and second-order optimization.

### Diagnostic Checkpoints & Authoritative Solutions

#### Checkpoint 1: The Asymptotic Stagnation of First-Order Optimizers (Adam)
*Question:* In deep learning, Adam is celebrated for its adaptive learning rates and robustness to noisy gradients. Why does Adam inevitably plateau at a coarse residual plateau ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}} \sim 10^{-3}$ to $10^{-4}$) when optimizing physics-informed loss functionals, and why can it not achieve engineering-grade precision ($10^{-7}$)?  
*Solution:* Adam computes parameter updates based on moving averages of first-order gradients: $\theta^{(t+1)} = \theta^{(t)} - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \epsilon} \hat{m}_t$. Near a local or global minimum, the gradient $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} \to \mathbf{0}$, but the second moment estimate $v_t$ retains a historical floor. More fundamentally, Adam approximates the local loss geometry as an isotropic or axis-aligned diagonal quadratic. However, physics-informed loss landscapes are characterized by highly elongated, curved ravines governed by the dense, non-diagonal Hessian matrix $\mathbf{H} = \nabla_\theta^2 \mathcal{L}$. First-order gradient updates oscillate violently across the steep ravine walls while making negligible progress along the flat base. Without curvature information (Hessian inverse $\mathbf{H}^{-1}$), first-order methods exhibit sublinear convergence $\mathcal{O}(1/t)$ or asymptotic stagnation.

#### Checkpoint 2: Quasi-Newton Optimization and the L-BFGS Secant Equation
*Question:* How does the Limited-memory Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (L-BFGS) algorithm approximate the inverse Hessian matrix $\mathbf{H}^{-1}$ without explicitly forming or storing an $\mathcal{O}(P^2)$ memory matrix?  
*Solution:* L-BFGS enforces the *quasi-Newton secant equation*:
$$\mathbf{B}_{k+1} \mathbf{s}_k = \mathbf{y}_k \iff \mathbf{H}_{k+1} \mathbf{y}_k = \mathbf{s}_k$$
where parameter displacement $\mathbf{s}_k = \theta_{k+1} - \theta_k$ and gradient change $\mathbf{y}_k = \nabla \mathcal{L}_{k+1} - \nabla \mathcal{L}_k$. Rather than storing the full dense $P \times P$ Hessian matrix (which requires $400\text{ GB}$ for $P = 10^5$ parameters), L-BFGS stores only the $m$ most recent vector pairs $\{(\mathbf{s}_i, \mathbf{y}_i)\}_{i=k-m}^{k-1}$ (typically history size $m \in [10, 50]$). Using the **two-loop recursion algorithm**, L-BFGS computes the exact Newton-like descent direction $\mathbf{p}_k = -\mathbf{H}_k \nabla \mathcal{L}_k$ in $\mathcal{O}(m P)$ operations and memory. This captures non-diagonal parameter coupling and exhibits superlinear convergence rates.

#### Checkpoint 3: The Strong Wolfe Line Search Conditions
*Question:* PyTorch’s `torch.optim.LBFGS` requires passing `line_search_fn="strong_wolfe"`. State the two mathematical inequalities comprising the Strong Wolfe conditions and explain why they are critical for maintaining positive-definite Hessian updates.  
*Solution:* A step length $\alpha_k > 0$ along descent direction $\mathbf{p}_k$ satisfies the Strong Wolfe conditions if:
1. **Armijo Sufficient Decrease Condition:**
   $$\mathcal{L}(\theta_k + \alpha_k \mathbf{p}_k) \le \mathcal{L}(\theta_k) + c_1 \alpha_k \nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_k)^T \mathbf{p}_k$$
   where $0 < c_1 < 1$ (typically $c_1 = 10^{-4}$). This guarantees that the loss decreases proportional to the predicted linear slope.
2. **Curvature Condition:**
   $$\left| \nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_k + \alpha_k \mathbf{p}_k)^T \mathbf{p}_k \right| \le c_2 \left| \nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_k)^T \mathbf{p}_k \right|$$
   where $c_1 < c_2 < 1$ (typically $c_2 = 0.9$).  
*Significance:* The curvature condition guarantees that $\mathbf{s}_k^T \mathbf{y}_k > 0$. This inner product positivity is the necessary and sufficient condition for the BFGS update formula to preserve positive-definiteness in the inverse Hessian approximation $\mathbf{H}_{k+1} \succ 0$, ensuring that all future search directions $\mathbf{p}_{k+1} = -\mathbf{H}_{k+1} \nabla \mathcal{L}_{k+1}$ remain strictly descent directions.

#### Checkpoint 4: System Identifiability in Inverse Problems
*Question:* In an inverse problem where physical parameters $\boldsymbol{\lambda} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ are learned alongside state $u_\theta(\mathbf{x})$, what is *parameter identifiability*? Under what sensor configuration does an inverse PINN fail to identify parameters uniquely?  
*Solution:* A physical parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\lambda}^*$ is *identifiable* if distinct parameter values generate distinct observable state fields: $\boldsymbol{\lambda}_1 \neq \boldsymbol{\lambda}_2 \implies u(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\lambda}_1) \neq u(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\lambda}_2)$ across the sensor observation set $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}$.  
*Failure Modes:*  
1. *Trivial Nullspace / Uniform States:* If an unforced diffusion system $\partial_t u = \alpha \partial_{xx} u$ has relaxed to a uniform constant temperature $u(x, t) = T_0$, then $\partial_t u = 0$ and $\partial_{xx} u = 0$. The residual evaluates to $0 = \alpha \cdot 0$, which holds identically for *any* value of $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$. The parameter is completely unidentifiable.  
2. *Degenerate Sensor Siting:* If all sensors are placed exclusively at nodal points of an acoustic standing wave where displacement is permanently zero ($u(x_0, t) = 0, \forall t$), the sensor data provides zero information about wave speed $c$, making $c$ unidentifiable. Parameter discovery requires sensor placement in regions of non-zero spatiotemporal curvature.

#### Checkpoint 5: Neural Operators vs. Physics-Informed Neural Networks
*Question:* Contrast Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) against Fourier Neural Operators (FNO, Li et al., 2020) in terms of mapping architecture, generalization across boundary conditions, and training data requirements.  
*Solution:*\
- **PINNs (Function Surrogates):** A PINN parameterizes a single continuous solution field $u: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^m$ for one specific set of boundary conditions and geometry. It solves a specific instance of a PDE without requiring external training data. If boundary conditions, source terms, or geometries change, the entire network must be retrained from scratch.\
- **Neural Operators (Operator Surrogates):** An FNO learns an infinite-dimensional mapping between function spaces: $\mathcal{G}_\theta: \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{U}$, mapping an entire input function (e.g., initial condition $u_0(x) \in \mathcal{A}$ or permeability field $a(x)$) to the corresponding solution field $u(x, t) \in \mathcal{U}$. Once trained on a large dataset of simulated solutions, an FNO performs zero-shot inference across arbitrary new boundary conditions in milliseconds. However, FNOs require massive offline training datasets generated by classical solvers.

## 3. Core Technical Exposition

### 3.1 The Two-Phase Optimization Protocol

The definitive training strategy in scientific machine learning is the **Two-Phase Protocol**:

$$\text{Phase 1: Global Adam Exploration} \quad \xrightarrow{\text{Loss Plateau}} \quad \text{Phase 2: Superlinear L-BFGS Descent}$$

![Adam vs L-BFGS Convergence](../assets/adam_vs_lbfgs_convergence.png)

**Figure 4.** Optimization trajectory benchmark: First-Order Pure Adam stagnation vs. Two-Phase Quasi-Newton acceleration. Pure Adam (red) makes rapid early progress but plateaus asymptotically at $\sim 5 \times 10^{-4}$ due to gradient ravine oscillation. When the solver transitions to second-order L-BFGS at epoch 500 (green), the quasi-Newton curvature approximation drives the physics residual down to machine precision ($\sim 10^{-7}$).

#### Phase 1: First-Order Exploration (Adam)
- **Objective:** Navigate the chaotic, highly non-convex early loss landscape.  
- **Hyperparameters:** Learning rate $\eta \in [10^{-3}, 10^{-2}]$, $\beta_1 = 0.9, \beta_2 = 0.999$.  
- **Dynamics:** Adam’s momentum and adaptive coordinate scaling prevent premature trapping in shallow saddle points and allow the network to escape poor random initializations. It rapidly aligns macroscopic features and boundary profiles.  
- **Termination Criterion:** Plateau in the rolling validation loss ($\frac{|\mathcal{L}^{(t)} - \mathcal{L}^{(t-50)}|}{\mathcal{L}^{(t)}} < 10^{-3}$).

#### Phase 2: Second-Order Fine-Tuning (L-BFGS)
- **Objective:** Exploit the local quadratic convexity of the loss basin to achieve superlinear descent.  
- **Hyperparameters:** `history_size=20`, `max_iter=50,000`, `tolerance_grad=1e-8`, `tolerance_change=1e-12`, `line_search_fn="strong_wolfe"`.  
- **Dynamics:** Because full-batch collocation points are evaluated, the loss function is deterministic and smooth. L-BFGS uses exact gradients to construct an effective inverse Hessian $\mathbf{H}_k^{-1}$, adjusting step sizes along stiff and soft directions simultaneously.

### 3.2 Inverse Parameter Discovery Formulation

In classical inverse problems, identifying unknown physical constants $\boldsymbol{\lambda} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ from noisy data is ill-posed and requires complex adjoint state equations. In a PINN, inverse discovery is formulated by promoting physical parameters to **trainable network parameters**:

$$\theta_{\mathrm{augmented}} = \{ \theta_{\mathrm{weights}}, \theta_{\mathrm{biases}}, \boldsymbol{\lambda} \}$$

Consider the 1D non-linear advection-diffusion equation with unknown kinematic viscosity $\nu$:
$$\mathcal{R}(u; \nu) = \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} - \nu \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = 0$$

Let $\nu$ be instantiated as an unconstrained or positivity-constrained PyTorch parameter:
```python
# Unconstrained parameter
self.nu = nn.Parameter(torch.tensor([0.1], dtype=torch.float32))

# Positivity-enforced parameter (nu > 0)
self.log_nu = nn.Parameter(torch.tensor([np.log(0.1)], dtype=torch.float32))
nu = torch.exp(self.log_nu)
```

The composite loss function simultaneously penalizes:
1. Data mismatch on $N_d$ sparse, noisy interior sensor probes: $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}} = \frac{1}{N_d}\sum |u_\theta(\mathbf{x}_d^i) - u_{\mathrm{meas}}^i|^2$.
2. Differential equation defect on $N_f$ unmonitored collocation points: $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}} = \frac{1}{N_f}\sum |\mathcal{R}(u_\theta; \nu)|^2$.

During backward propagation, the parameter gradient is evaluated automatically:
$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}}}{\partial \nu} = \frac{2}{N_f} \sum_{i=1}^{N_f} \mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x}_i; \nu) \cdot \left( -\frac{\partial^2 u_\theta}{\partial x^2}(\mathbf{x}_i) \right)$$
The optimizer updates $\nu$ along with the network weights $\theta$, driving $\nu$ directly toward the true physical constant.

## 4. Runnable Laboratory: Two-Phase Inverse Parameter Discovery

The following self-contained script demonstrates inverse parameter discovery for a dynamic decay process:
$$\frac{du}{dt} + \lambda u = 0, \quad u(0) = 1.0 \implies u(t) = e^{-\lambda t}$$
True parameter: $\lambda^* = 0.5000\text{ s}^{-1}$.

We collect $N = 20$ noisy observations in $t \in [0, 2]\text{ s}$ with $1\%$ Gaussian noise, initialize the learnable parameter at $\lambda_{\mathrm{init}} = 1.5000$ (a $300\%$ error), and execute Phase 1 Adam followed by Phase 2 L-BFGS.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import numpy as np

# Set deterministic seeds
torch.manual_seed(42)
np.random.seed(42)

print("=== PINN LAB W3D5: TWO-PHASE INVERSE PARAMETER DISCOVERY ===")

# 1. Ground truth physics: u(t) = exp(-0.5 * t)
lambda_true = 0.5000
t_obs = torch.linspace(0.0, 2.0, 20)[:, None]
u_clean = torch.exp(-lambda_true * t_obs)
u_noisy = u_clean + 0.01 * torch.randn_like(u_clean)

# 2. Learnable parameter lambda (initialized far from truth at 1.5000)
lambda_param = nn.Parameter(torch.tensor([1.5000], dtype=torch.float32))

# 3. Neural Network Surrogate
net = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Linear(1, 20),
    nn.Tanh(),
    nn.Linear(20, 20),
    nn.Tanh(),
    nn.Linear(20, 1)
)

t_colloc = torch.linspace(0.0, 2.0, 60)[:, None].requires_grad_(True)
t_zero = torch.tensor([[0.0]], dtype=torch.float32, requires_grad=True)

# Optimization parameters: network weights + discoverable physics constant
all_params = list(net.parameters()) + [lambda_param]
opt_adam = torch.optim.Adam(all_params, lr=1e-2)

print(f"True Physical Constant: lambda = {lambda_true:.4f}")
print(f"Initial Guess:          lambda = {lambda_param.item():.4f} (Error: {abs(lambda_param.item()-lambda_true)/lambda_true*100:.1f}%)")
print("-" * 75)

# --- PHASE 1: ADAM FIRST-ORDER EXPLORATION ---
for epoch in range(400):
    opt_adam.zero_grad()
    loss_data = torch.mean((net(t_obs) - u_noisy)**2)
    
    u_c = net(t_colloc)
    u_t = torch.autograd.grad(u_c, t_colloc, torch.ones_like(u_c), create_graph=True)[0]
    res_ode = u_t + lambda_param * u_c
    loss_ode = torch.mean(res_ode**2)
    
    u_0 = net(t_zero)
    loss_ic = (u_0 - 1.0)**2
    
    loss_total = 20.0 * loss_data + loss_ode + 10.0 * loss_ic
    loss_total.backward()
    opt_adam.step()

loss_p1 = loss_total.item()
lambda_p1 = lambda_param.item()
err_p1 = abs(lambda_p1 - lambda_true) / lambda_true * 100

print(f"Phase 1 (Adam 400 ep): Loss = {loss_p1:.6e} | Learned lambda = {lambda_p1:.4f} (Error: {err_p1:.2f}%)")

# --- PHASE 2: L-BFGS SECOND-ORDER CONVERGENCE ---
opt_lbfgs = torch.optim.LBFGS(all_params, max_iter=20, history_size=10, line_search_fn="strong_wolfe")

def closure():
    opt_lbfgs.zero_grad()
    loss_data = torch.mean((net(t_obs) - u_noisy)**2)
    u_c = net(t_colloc)
    u_t = torch.autograd.grad(u_c, t_colloc, torch.ones_like(u_c), create_graph=True)[0]
    res_ode = u_t + lambda_param * u_c
    loss_ode = torch.mean(res_ode**2)
    u_0 = net(t_zero)
    loss_ic = (u_0 - 1.0)**2
    loss = 20.0 * loss_data + loss_ode + 10.0 * loss_ic
    loss.backward()
    return loss

opt_lbfgs.step(closure)

loss_p2 = closure().item()
lambda_p2 = lambda_param.item()
err_p2 = abs(lambda_p2 - lambda_true) / lambda_true * 100

print(f"Phase 2 (L-BFGS fine): Loss = {loss_p2:.6e} | Learned lambda = {lambda_p2:.4f} (Error: {err_p2:.2f}%)")
```

### Exam Question 1: Coordinate Autodiff Complexity and Vector-Jacobian Products
*Problem:* Let $u_\theta(\mathbf{x}): \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^1$ be a PINN representing acoustic pressure where $\mathbf{x} = [x, y, t]^T$. We require the 2D acoustic wave residual $\mathcal{R} = \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} - c^2 \left( \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} \right) = 0$.  
1. Detail the exact sequence of PyTorch automatic differentiation calls required to assemble this residual tensor on a batch of $N_f$ collocation coordinates.  
2. If computing the forward pass $u_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ requires $F$ floating-point operations (FLOPs), compute the theoretical upper bound on the FLOP count required to evaluate the residual $\mathcal{R}$ on the batch using reverse-mode autodiff.

*Solution:*\
1. **PyTorch Code Sequence:**
   ```python
   # x has shape [N_f, 3] where col 0: x, col 1: y, col 2: t
   x.requires_grad_(True)
   u = model(x) # [N_f, 1]
   
   # First coordinate derivative vector [u_x, u_y, u_t] via single reverse pass
   grad_u = torch.autograd.grad(u, x, grad_outputs=torch.ones_like(u), create_graph=True)[0]
   u_x = grad_u[:, 0:1]
   u_y = grad_u[:, 1:2]
   u_t = grad_u[:, 2:3]
   
   # Second derivatives: each requires one coordinate backward pass
   u_xx = torch.autograd.grad(u_x, x, grad_outputs=torch.ones_like(u_x), create_graph=True)[0][:, 0:1]
   u_yy = torch.autograd.grad(u_y, x, grad_outputs=torch.ones_like(u_y), create_graph=True)[0][:, 1:2]
   u_tt = torch.autograd.grad(u_t, x, grad_outputs=torch.ones_like(u_t), create_graph=True)[0][:, 2:3]
   
   residual = u_tt - (c**2) * (u_xx + u_yy)
   ```
2. **Computational FLOP Complexity:**  
   - Baur-Strassen Theorem: Reverse-mode automatic differentiation computes the gradient of a scalar output with respect to all inputs in $\le 4 \times \text{Cost}(\text{Forward Pass})$.  
   - Forward pass: $F$ FLOPs.  
   - First derivative vector $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} u$: $1$ reverse sweep $\le 4 F$ FLOPs.  
   - Second derivatives: Three scalar fields ($u_x, u_y, u_t$) are differentiated with respect to $\mathbf{x}$. Each reverse sweep takes $\le 4 \times \text{Cost}(\text{Graph of } u_k)$. Since $\text{Cost}(u_k) \le 5 F$, each second derivative requires $\le 4 \times (5F) = 20 F$ FLOPs.  
   - Total evaluation cost: $\text{Cost}(\mathcal{R}) \le F + 4F + 3 \times (20F) \approx 65 F$ FLOPs.  
   While substantial, this cost is purely a small constant multiplier ($\approx 65\times$) that does not scale with grid resolution or dimension $d$.

### Exam Question 2: Latin Hypercube Stratification and Probability Measure
*Problem:* In a 2D spatial domain $\Omega = [0, 1]^2$, $N$ collocation points are drawn via Latin Hypercube Sampling.  
1. Prove that the probability that any two distinct points $\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j$ share the same horizontal or vertical stratum of width $\frac{1}{N}$ is identically zero.  
2. If $N = 100$ points are instead sampled uniformly at random via standard Monte Carlo, compute the probability that at least one coordinate stratum of width $\Delta x = \frac{1}{100}$ receives two or more points (the Birthday Paradox in spatial sampling).

*Solution:*\
1. **LHS Stratum Exclusivity:**  
   In LHS, the coordinate assignment for dimension $k$ is given by $x_{i, k} = \frac{\pi_k(i) - 1 + U_{i, k}}{N}$, where $\pi_k$ is a strict bijection (permutation) from $\{1, \dots, N\} \to \{1, \dots, N\}$. For any two distinct indices $i \neq j$, by definition of a permutation, $\pi_k(i) \neq \pi_k(j)$. Therefore, $x_{i, k} \in \left[\frac{\pi_k(i)-1}{N}, \frac{\pi_k(i)}{N}\right)$ and $x_{j, k} \in \left[\frac{\pi_k(j)-1}{N}, \frac{\pi_k(j)}{N}\right)$ belong to completely disjoint intervals. The probability of stratum collision is strictly zero: $P(\text{Collision}) = 0$.
2. **Monte Carlo Spatial Collision (Birthday Problem):**  
   Let $M = 100$ bins and $N = 100$ independent random particles.  
   The probability that all $N$ particles land in distinct bins is:
   $$P(\text{No Collision}) = \prod_{k=0}^{N-1} \left( \frac{M - k}{M} \right) = \frac{M!}{M^N} = \frac{100!}{100^{100}}$$
   Using Stirling's approximation $n! \approx \sqrt{2\pi n} \left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^n$:
   $$P(\text{No Collision}) \approx \sqrt{2\pi(100)} \left(\frac{100}{e}\right)^{100} \frac{1}{100^{100}} = \sqrt{200\pi} e^{-100} \approx 25.066 \times 3.72 \times 10^{-44} \approx 9.32 \times 10^{-43}$$
   The probability of at least one spatial collision is:
   $$P(\text{At least one collision}) = 1 - P(\text{No Collision}) = 1 - 9.32 \times 10^{-43} \approx 1.00000000$$
   Under standard random sampling, it is an absolute mathematical certainty that points will cluster into duplicate strata while leaving other regions empty. LHS completely eliminates this clustering pathology.

### Exam Question 3: Gradient Pathologies in Stiff Reaction-Diffusion Systems
*Problem:* Consider the steady Allen-Cahn reaction-diffusion equation with interface parameter $\epsilon = 0.01$:
$$\epsilon \frac{d^2 u}{dx^2} - (u^3 - u) = 0, \quad x \in [-1, 1], \quad u(-1) = -1, \quad u(1) = 1$$
1. Describe the physical structure of the solution and the width of the phase transition layer at $x = 0$.  
2. If this problem is solved using a standard PINN with fixed loss weights $\lambda_{\mathrm{pde}} = 1.0, \lambda_{\mathrm{bc}} = 1.0$, explain why the optimizer converges to the unphysical trivial state $u_\theta(x) = 0$ or an unphysical linear ramp.  
3. Formulate how Learning Rate Annealing and Residual-Based Adaptive Distribution work together to resolve this stiff problem.

*Solution:*\
1. **Physical Solution Structure:**  
   The Allen-Cahn equation models phase separation between two stable thermodynamic phases ($u = -1$ and $u = +1$). The non-linear potential $V(u) = \frac{1}{4}(u^2 - 1)^2$ drives $u$ toward $\pm 1$. The analytical solution exhibits a sharp hyperbolic tangent interface centered at $x = 0$:
   $$u(x) = \tanh\left( \frac{x}{\sqrt{2}\epsilon} \right)$$
   The interface transition layer width is $\delta \sim \sqrt{2}\epsilon = \sqrt{2}(0.01) \approx 0.0141$, spanning less than $0.7\%$ of the domain $[-1, 1]$.
2. **Failure of Naïve PINN Optimization:**  
   - In the bulk domain ($|x| > 0.05$), $u \approx \pm 1$, so $u^3 - u \approx 0$ and $u'' \approx 0$.  
   - If the network initializes near zero ($u_\theta \approx 0$), the cubic reaction term $u^3 - u \approx 0$ and curvature $u'' \approx 0$. The interior PDE residual is trivially satisfied ($\mathcal{R} \approx 0$).  
   - Developing the sharp phase boundary requires the network to generate extreme second derivatives: $u''(x) \sim \frac{1}{\epsilon^2} = 10^4$ at the interface. Introducing high curvature temporarily inflates $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}}$ by orders of magnitude. The optimizer encounters a massive energy barrier in the loss landscape, trapping $u_\theta$ in an unphysical state that satisfies the boundary conditions but ignores the phase separation physics.
3. **Synergistic Algorithmic Resolution:**  
   - *Residual-Based Adaptive Distribution (RAD):* Evaluates the residual across candidate points, detects the huge defect at the interface, and clusters $> 80\%$ of collocation points within $[-0.02, 0.02]$, providing dense resolution where $u'' \sim 10^4$.  
   - *Learning Rate Annealing:* Dynamically boosts the interior PDE weight $\lambda_{\mathrm{pde}}$ relative to the boundary penalty, forcing parameter gradients to overcome the reaction potential barrier and establish the sharp phase transition.

### Exam Question 4: L-BFGS Two-Loop Recursion Mechanics
*Problem:* In Phase 2 optimization, the L-BFGS two-loop recursion computes the search direction $\mathbf{r} = -\mathbf{H}_k \mathbf{g}_k$ using stored displacement vectors $\mathbf{s}_i = \theta_{i+1} - \theta_i$ and gradient difference vectors $\mathbf{y}_i = \mathbf{g}_{i+1} - \mathbf{g}_i$ for $i = k-1, \dots, k-m$.  
1. Write the pseudocode for the L-BFGS two-loop recursion algorithm.  
2. If the network contains $P = 50,000$ trainable parameters and history size $m = 20$, compute the total floating-point storage required for the memory buffers in single precision.

*Solution:*\
1. **L-BFGS Two-Loop Recursion Algorithm:**
   ```text
   Algorithm: L-BFGS Two-Loop Recursion
   Input: Current gradient g_k, memory history {(s_i, y_i)} for i = k-1 down to k-m
   
   q = g_k
   For i = k-1 down to k-m:
       rho_i = 1.0 / (y_i^T s_i)
       alpha_i = rho_i * (s_i^T q)
       q = q - alpha_i * y_i
   
   // Initial Hessian scaling
   gamma_k = (s_{k-1}^T y_{k-1}) / (y_{k-1}^T y_{k-1})
   r = gamma_k * q
   
   For i = k-m up to k-1:
       rho_i = 1.0 / (y_i^T s_i)
       beta = rho_i * (y_i^T r)
       r = r + s_i * (alpha_i - beta)
   
   Return search direction p_k = -r
   ```
2. **Buffer Storage Calculation:**  
   - Each vector $\mathbf{s}_i$ and $\mathbf{y}_i$ contains $P = 50,000$ floating-point values.  
   - In 32-bit single precision, each float requires $4$ bytes: $50,000 \times 4\text{ bytes} = 200,000\text{ bytes} = 200\text{ KB}$ per vector.  
   - For history size $m = 20$, the buffer stores $m$ displacement vectors $\mathbf{s}$ and $m$ gradient difference vectors $\mathbf{y}$:
     $$\text{Total Storage} = 2 \times m \times P \times 4\text{ bytes} = 2 \times 20 \times 200\text{ KB} = 8,000\text{ KB} = 8.0\text{ MB}$$
   L-BFGS achieves full second-order curvature approximation using just **$8.0\text{ MB}$** of RAM, whereas the explicit dense Hessian matrix would require $P^2 \times 4\text{ bytes} = (50,000)^2 \times 4 = 10^{10}\text{ bytes} = 10.0\text{ GB}$.

### Exam Question 5: D'Alembert Exact Solution vs. PINN Representation
*Problem:* The 1D wave equation $\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = c^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$ on the infinite line $x \in (-\infty, \infty)$ with initial displacement $u(x, 0) = f(x)$ and initial velocity $u_t(x, 0) = 0$ has the classical D'Alembert analytical solution:
$$u(x, t) = \frac{1}{2} \left[ f(x - ct) + f(x + ct) \right]$$
1. Explain how a PINN with $\text{Tanh}$ activation functions represents this non-dispersive wave propagation.  
2. If $f(x)$ is a discontinuous square wave, why does the PINN exhibit Gibbs-like oscillations and numerical dispersion, and how does this relate to the spectral bias of deep networks?

*Solution:*\
1. **PINN Representation of D'Alembert Wave:**  
   A feedforward neural network computes $u_\theta(x, t) = \sum_{j} W_{2, j} \tanh(w_{1, j}^x x + w_{1, j}^t t + b_j)$.  
   To satisfy the wave equation, the network must align its first-layer weights such that the ratio of temporal to spatial weights matches the wave propagation speed:
   $$\left| \frac{w_{1, j}^t}{w_{1, j}^x} \right| = c$$
   When this condition holds, each hidden neuron computes a function of the characteristic coordinates: $\tanh\left( w_{1, j}^x (x \pm c t) + b_j \right)$. The output layer linearly combines these traveling hyperbolic tangent profiles, faithfully synthesizing the left- and right-propagating D'Alembert waveforms without numerical dissipation.
2. **Spectral Bias and Gibbs Pathology:**  
   - According to the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) spectral analysis (Rahaman et al., 2019), standard neural networks exhibit *spectral bias*: they learn low-frequency Fourier components at exponential rates while requiring exponentially many iterations to resolve high frequencies.  
   - A discontinuous square wave contains an infinite Fourier spectrum decaying slowly as $\mathcal{O}(1/k)$.  
   - Because the PINN cannot immediately resolve the high-wavenumber modes, truncating the infinite series in Fourier space causes the *Gibbs phenomenon*: severe $\approx 9\%$ overshoots and high-frequency ringing near the discontinuity.  
   - Furthermore, because coordinate autograd evaluates smooth derivatives $\sigma''(z)$, enforcing the differential equation across the jump attempts to evaluate undefined Dirac delta derivatives $\delta'(x \pm ct)$, destabilizing optimization unless specialized architectures (such as SIREN or domain decomposition XPINNs) are employed.
